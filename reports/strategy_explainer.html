Kalman-Filtered Pairs Spread · Plain-English Walkthrough

Betting that two related stocks snap back together, explained step by step

This strategy finds two economically-linked stocks, tracks the gap between their prices, and bets that when the gap gets unusually wide, it will shrink back to normal. Below: the mechanism in plain English, the actual math turned into pictures, a real trade that worked, a real trade that didn't, and how it's performed on data none of this was built or tuned on.

On testing data never used to fit the model (2024-01-01 to 2026-08-21, 661 trading days): turned $1000 into $1,270 (Sharpe 3.83), with -0.026 correlation to the S&P 500 — essentially zero.

The five-step mechanism

STEP 1

Pick two linked stocks

Two companies whose businesses move together for real economic reasons — e.g. two payment networks, two beverage makers. Screened and statistically confirmed, not assumed.

STEP 2

Track the gap between them

The "spread" = one stock's price minus a ratio (β) of the other's. That ratio drifts over time, so it's re-estimated every day (a Kalman filter) rather than fixed once.

STEP 3

Filter out the noise

The raw gap is jittery. Break it into frequency "waves" (FFT), keep only the strongest ones, discard the rest — leaving a cleaner picture of the real pattern.

STEP 4

Wait for it to stretch far

Compare today's (cleaned) gap to its own recent normal range. When it's stretched unusually far in either direction, that's the signal.

STEP 5

Bet on the snap-back

Buy the cheap side, sell the expensive side, in the exact ratio that makes the bet neutral to the overall market. Close the position once the gap normalizes.

A real losing trade — why it doesn't always work

Not every trade reverts on schedule. This is the actual mechanism failing, not a hypothetical: the spread kept drifting instead of snapping back, and the position was closed at a loss when the z-score itself unwound past our exit band on the wrong side, rather than reverting to the entry level.

$-2.44 on $250 allocated
  • ENTRYOn 2023-10-05, z-score was -1.47, past the entry threshold. Decision: LONG the spread (buy MA, sell V). V at $228.22, MA at $387.79, β=1.706.
  • WHAT WENT WRONGRather than reverting toward the entry level, the spread continued moving and eventually crossed back the other way, past the exit band on the losing side — the relationship didn't behave the way its recent history suggested it would.
  • EXITClosed on 2023-11-13 for $-2.44. This is the real, irreducible risk: mean reversion is a tendency estimated from history, not a guarantee for any single trade.

Why near-zero correlation is the actual point

SPY earned far more in raw terms over this same window (66.2% vs. 27.0%) — this was never going to out-earn a strong bull market, and it shouldn't be judged as if that were the goal. What it does instead: its daily returns have essentially no relationship to the market's (correlation -0.026), confirmed individually for every pair including the more cyclical airline pair (LUV/JBLU: -0.0153). A return stream this independent of market direction is valuable specifically as a diversifier — it doesn't need to beat the market to be worth holding alongside it.
