Current verdict: V/MA and KO/PEP only, Sharpe ~1.9, ~4.5%/yr unlevered, validated across two independent out-of-sample periods (2022–2023 and 2024–2026) — not a SPY replacement, a near-zero-correlation diversifier that held up during SPY's three worst drawdowns in this window

Why it fails, and what to fix

  1. The chain of cause is traceable, not mysterious. Phase 1's likelihood-optimal noise parameters push the filter into an over-adaptive regime → the residual spread has a half-life under one day at every percentile tested → a signal that reverts in under a day, traded on daily bars, cannot generate enough magnitude between entries and exits to outrun a realistic ~5bps-per-leg cost against real notional.
  2. Denoising alone can't rescue it. Raising the FFT percentile threshold shrinks trade count and losses (V/MA: −15.5 max DD at p=0 → −3.1 at p=95) but never flips Sharpe positive — it's suppressing the same too-fast signal, not fixing its root cause.
  3. The fix is upstream, in Phase 1 — tested below, not just proposed. Re-fit the Kalman noise parameters with a selection criterion that penalizes short implied half-life, not raw log-likelihood alone.

The fix, tested: constrain the fit away from the degenerate regime

Added a half-life floor to the Phase 1 noise-parameter search: among (delta, obs_cov) grid points, maximize log-likelihood only among those whose resulting spread has OU half-life ≥ 10 days, instead of maximizing log-likelihood alone.

SPY/VOO has the tightest cointegration in the entire 36-pair universe (p < 0.02) but Sharpe of −7.75 and −5.96 out-of-sample — near-perfect cointegration between two funds tracking the same index leaves almost no real signal, just costs and noise. With 36 candidates tested, the Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold is p < 0.00139 — almost nothing survives it. Raw p<0.05 alone is not a reliable filter at this many candidates; repeated appearance across independent folds with consistent-sign OOS performance is what's actually separating signal from noise here.

Leverage: the real lever is financing cost, not risk appetite

Leveraging an uncorrelated, positive-Sharpe strategy is the correct instinct behind risk-parity and portable-alpha investing. Whether it works here depends almost entirely on what rate you borrow at.

⚠
At 7% retail margin, leverage is a bad trade — financing drag erodes Sharpe faster than it grows return. At 3% or below, leverage is genuinely attractive: 3x at institutional-grade financing reaches ~12%/yr with Sharpe still above 1.6. The catch is access, not math — retail accounts don't get sub-3% financing; that requires portfolio-margin or futures-based structures that typically need six-figure account equity, or real institutional access. At $1000, leverage is not the available lever; growing the capital base (or the statistical confidence in the edge) is.

●
Full Kelly sizing estimates 77x leverage as "optimal" — that's a warning sign, not a target. Kelly assumes free borrowing (it ignores the financing table above entirely) and is extremely sensitive to estimation error in the mean return, which is exactly what a small, noisy sample like this one has plenty of — the walk-forward section above just demonstrated real fragility in what we thought was validated. Sizing bets by a formula that's this sensitive to an uncertain input isn't optimal, it's fragile.

The honestly correct read: current sizing is simple and arbitrary, not derived from any formal optimality criterion — but given real, just-demonstrated uncertainty in the edge itself, arbitrary-and-conservative is the defensible choice right now. The principled next step (a small, fractional-Kelly, signal-strength-scaled sizing rule) is worth building only after paper trading gives a live, harder-to-overfit estimate of the true edge — optimizing bet sizing on a still-uncertain edge just compounds the same estimation-error problem Kelly already exposed.
